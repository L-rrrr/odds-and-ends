What happens when you type a website's name + tech, explainedoct 2026

Computers don't know names; they know numbers. So the first thing your browser does is ask a DNS server, "what number belongs to this name?" It gets back an IP address.

Then it opens a connection to that address, agrees on encryption (that's the "s" in https), and asks for the page. The server sends back HTML, which tells the browser what else to fetch: styles, scripts, images. Each of those is another request.

Like asking a friend for someone's address, walking there, knocking, and being handed a letter that lists more places to visit.

Big-O, without the maths + tech, explainedoct 2026

Big-O isn't about how fast something is. It's about how much slower it gets when the input grows.

O(n): twice the data, twice the work. O(n²): twice the data, four times the work. O(log n): twice the data, one extra step. O(1): the data size doesn't matter at all.

Finding a name in a phone book by reading every page is O(n). Opening it in the middle and halving each time is O(log n).

Bread doesn't go stale by drying out + anything elseoct 2026

Stale bread can have almost as much water as fresh bread. What changes is the starch: when bread cools, starch molecules slowly line up and crystallise again, which makes it firm. This is called retrogradation.

It's also why the fridge makes bread go stale faster, and why warming stale bread briefly softens it: heat undoes some of the crystallising.

Why the sky is blue but sunsets aren't + anything elseoct 2026

Air scatters short wavelengths (blue) much more than long ones (red). During the day, scattered blue light reaches you from every direction, so the whole sky looks blue.

At sunset, light travels through far more air to reach you. Most of the blue gets scattered away before it arrives, leaving the oranges and reds.
